Methodology and data

How every number is made

Start with the plain-English version, follow one town all the way through, then open any section to see the exact formula and source for each number you see on the map.

In plain English

The ideaA town is more affordable when homes cost less compared with what people there earn, when prices aren't racing upward, and when local zoning rules leave room to build more homes. We turn those three ideas into three numbers, average them, and get one score from 0 to 100. Higher means more affordable.
CollectHome values, incomes, and zoning scores for each town from public sources.
MeasureWork out price burden, price growth, and zoning for every town.
CompareTurn each measure into a 0–100 "affordability percentile" against all other towns.
CombineTake a weighted average of the three. You choose the weights.

No hidden adjustments are applied. Each source is used as published, and everything we add on top is on this page.

Home value (ZHVI)

What a typical home in town is worth.

Town home value

Zillow Home Value Index
Source: Zillow Research, monthly series from January 2000, all homes (single-family and condos) in the middle price tier. We use the September value of each year.
In plain EnglishZillow estimates the typical value of homes in the middle of the market, from its Zestimate model. It is smoothed and seasonally adjusted, so it is steadier than a median of actual sales.

Zillow publishes values for neighborhood-sized areas. Several of these can sit inside one municipality, so each town's value is the simple average of its Zillow areas (not weighted by population or sales):

ValueVtown = V1 + V2 + … + Vkk

Household income

What households in town earn.

Median household income

Census ACS
Source: U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2019–2023 5-year estimates, table B19013, by county subdivision.
In plain EnglishHalf the households in the town earn more than this amount, and half earn less. Five-year estimates are steadier for small towns than one-year ones.

We use the figure as published, with no adjustment. Two things to know: the Census reports it in 2023 dollars, and it caps very high incomes at $250,001 (10 towns hit that cap, such as Millburn), which makes those towns look slightly less affordable than they are. Missing or suppressed values are left blank.

Zoning restrictiveness

How strongly local rules limit new housing.

Zoning Restrictiveness Index (ZRI)

NZLUD
Source: National Zoning and Land Use Database, Mleczko & Desmond (Princeton / Eviction Lab). Published in Urban Studies.
In plain EnglishThe authors read municipal zoning codes with software and condensed rules like minimum lot sizes, density limits, parking requirements, and height limits into a single number. Higher means more exclusionary zoning.

The ZRI is a standardized score (an average town is about 0), so a negative value means more permissive than average. We do not recompute the ZRI. We use the value the authors published and only convert it to a percentile. The full construction is in the authors' paper (see the link below).

Example: NewarkZRI = 0.88, which is less restrictive than about 96% of the 105 NJ towns that have a ZRI.

Zoning data exists for 105 of NJ's 564 towns. The other zoning details in each town's panel (minimum lot size, inclusionary zoning, accessory units, parking, share zoned multifamily) are shown as coded in NZLUD and are not part of the score.

Percentiles

How we put different units on the same scale.

In plain EnglishPrice burden is a ratio, growth is a percent, and zoning is a standardized score, so they can't be averaged directly. A percentile just asks: out of all towns, where does this one rank? Then we flip it so that 100 always means "most affordable."
Percentilep(x) = rank of x among all townsnumber of towns with data × 100
Ties share the average rank.
P100 − p(price burden)
G100 − p(5-year price growth)
Z100 − p(zoning ZRI)

We subtract from 100 because a higher burden, faster growth, or stricter zoning all mean less affordable. The percentile is computed separately for each factor, among only the towns that have it: 461 towns for P, 441 for G, 105 for Z.

Why percentiles instead of raw values? They stop one extreme town (a $4.6 million average home, for example) from stretching the scale for everyone else, and they put all three factors on the same 0–100 footing before weighting. The trade-off is that a percentile shows rank, not size of the gap.

Final score and rank

The weighted average, and what happens when data is missing.

ScorewP·P + wG·G + wZ·ZwP + wG + wZ

The default weights are wP = 50, wG = 25, wZ = 25. They are relative, so 50/25/25 and 2/1/1 give the same score. A slider set to 0 removes that factor entirely.

Example: Newark(50 × 7.6 + 25 × 96.1 + 25 × 96.2) ÷ 100 = 51.9

When a factor is missing

If a town has no zoning data, we drop that factor and divide by the weights that remain, so the score stays on a 0–100 scale:

No zoning data: (50 × P + 25 × G) ÷ (50 + 25)     No growth or zoning: P only

A town must have price burden (P) to be scored. Nothing is estimated or filled in. Rank is simply each town's position when all scored towns are sorted by score, 1 being most affordable. It changes whenever you change the weights.

Combining Redfin areas into one town

Redfin reports places such as "Colonia" or "Iselin" that Zillow and the state treat as part of one municipality. We matched 414 NJ Redfin places to 387 municipalities using the same Zillow-area crosswalk plus unique official names. Places with no clean match (unincorporated localities) are left out rather than guessed. Redfin's newest files are monthly, so for each place we first average May, June and July (prices, days and ratios weighted by each month's sales). When several Redfin places belong to one town:

Matching towns across sources

Why one town isn't always one row.

Every dataset names places differently, so we join them on the official NJ municipal code (and the Census county-subdivision ID for income). Several place names are shared by two municipalities, usually a borough and a township. Each was resolved by cross-referencing Zillow's region ID with the official municipal code list:

Zillow regionRegion IDResolved to
Boonton3752Boonton Town (1401). "Boonton Township" (17117) already holds code 1402.
Andover831875Andover Township (1902), matching NZLUD's record.
Berlin760887Berlin Borough (405). "West Berlin" (21762) holds township code 406.
Clinton10869Clinton Town (1005).
Shrewsbury397046Shrewsbury Borough (1344), matching NZLUD's record.

Some municipalities, like Long Beach Township, are split into separate pieces on the map. They are still one town with one set of numbers.

Limitations

  • Owners only. Zillow's index reflects home values, not rents, so the score says nothing directly about renter affordability.
  • Missing Zillow areas. Zillow no longer publishes Long Valley, so Washington Township (Morris County), which relied on it, has no home value and is not scored.
  • Income precision. ACS incomes have margins of error that grow in small towns, and 10 towns are capped at $250,001.
  • Thin zoning coverage. NZLUD covers about 19% of NJ towns. For the rest the score leans on price burden and price growth.
  • Equal averaging. Zillow areas inside one town are averaged equally, not by population.
  • Market data is context. Redfin figures are not in the score, and small-town values can rest on a handful of sales.
  • Percentiles show rank, not distance. Two towns 1 point apart may be very close or not, depending on the data around them.
On weighting. The 50/25/25 default is a judgment call, not a derived optimum. The sliders exist so you can test your own.
On precision. The index is a weighted average, not a precision instrument. Treat a single score, or a small gap between two towns, as a rough estimate. The underlying data is used as published, and each source documents its own methodology (below).
